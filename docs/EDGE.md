## Gaps in the field

- **No product serves the 1:1 / people-side of the job. An EM's real weekly agenda is per-person — what did Anika actually ship, what is she stuck on, what did she commit to last time, has anyone been carrying every incident — and the entire category refuses to produce a per-person view on principle. So the manager keeps a private per-report doc forever and re-derives the same facts by hand before every 1:1.** — real pain
  - Who: Every EM of 1–3 teams, weekly, in the 15 minutes before each 1:1; sharpest for new managers who don't yet have the context in their head.
  - Evidence: The most universal artifact-kept-alongside-the-product in this job is a private Google Doc or Notion page per direct report, manually updated. Delivery tools give team aggregates; the manager needs a person and has to assemble it from Jira filters, GitHub search, and memory. No product in the field ships a 1:1 prep brief.
  - Still unserved because: This is a genuine tension, not an oversight. Per-developer views are the category's third rail — Pluralsight Flow's leaderboards became the cautionary tale, and Swarmia's whole go-to-market is 'we don't do individual ranking.' The stance that buys adoption forbids the artifact managers most want. The unserved middle — per-person context that is private to the manager, developmental rather than comparative, and never rolls up to leadership — is available precisely because incumbents took an absolute position instead of a designed one. Doing it requires a hard commitment (no cross-person comparison, no export upward, visible to the engineer) that costs enterprise deals.
- **The manager cannot tell the system anything. Every product is pull-only from artifacts, so the facts that exist only in the manager's head — Priya is interviewing all week, the contractor's last day is Friday, the PM verbally descoped that epic, legal is the actual blocker — never enter the model. The report then confidently asserts things the manager knows are false, and there is no way to correct it once and have it stick.** — dealbreaker
  - Who: Every manager, from about day three, when the report calls someone stalled who is on holiday or flags off-goal work that the PM explicitly requested.
  - Evidence: Metrics tools have no write path at all beyond configuration. Standup bots take free text but have no model to update, so the same context must be retyped every day. The workaround is that the manager mentally discounts sections of the report — which is the beginning of not reading it.
  - Still unserved because: Dashboards are a read-only architecture: a knowledge model you can write to in prose is unbounded, hard to validate, and creates a second source of truth the vendor must reconcile against Jira. Vendors avoid it because a manager-asserted fact that contradicts the tracker turns the product into an argument. But this is the difference between a system with memory and a report generator, and accept/reject on recommendations is not a substitute for stating a fact.
- **The engineer whose work gets flagged as off-goal has no right of reply. Goal-alignment verdicts are rendered about a named person's commits, delivered to their manager, and the person named never sees the verdict, cannot attach the reason ('the PM asked me to', 'this unblocks the migration'), and cannot contest a wrong one.** — dealbreaker
  - Who: ICs, the moment they learn the tool exists; and the manager, who must either defend a verdict they didn't write or stop trusting the section.
  - Evidence: Alignment/investment-allocation features across the field (Jellyfish initiative buckets, Swarmia investment balance) are one-directional: they classify engineers' work for management consumption with no channel back. The predictable outcome is the surveillance objection, and the manager's workaround is to never mention where the flag came from.
  - Still unserved because: The buyer is management, so the product surface is management-facing by default; building a contest channel means admitting the classifier is often wrong and giving non-paying users veto power over paid output. It also slows the report. Left unbuilt, it is the single most likely cause of organized resistance to a goal-alignment product — which makes it a moat, not a nicety.
- **Nobody audits whether the team's own process data means anything. The one thing a small team most needs told — 'your story points have had no relationship to elapsed days for four sprints; the projection below is really a cycle-time guess', or 'six of your last ten sprints ended with 30% carryover, so committed scope is fiction' — is never said, because saying it invalidates the vendor's own velocity and forecast charts.** — real pain
  - Who: Managers of 1–3 teams with the messiest Jira in the industry — no estimation discipline, inconsistent workflows, sub-tasks used inconsistently — i.e. most of the target market.
  - Evidence: Every product in the field is documented as 'only as good as your Jira hygiene', and each responds by asking the customer to improve hygiene or by tagging taxonomies up front. None turns the analysis on the process itself and reports the measurement error. Detecting a stale ticket is now common; reporting that the estimation ritual is decorative is not.
  - Still unserved because: Directly against the incumbent business model. Jellyfish, LinearB and Swarmia sell numbers; a module whose output is 'your numbers are noise' undermines the rest of the product and insults the buyer's process in the same breath. A challenger with one prose report and no charts to defend can say it, and it is the most credibility-buying sentence available in this category.
- **The most common real blocker for a 1–3 team manager is a dependency outside their boundary — the platform team, design, security review, a vendor API, another manager's roadmap — and every product scopes strictly to the repos and boards you own. External waits therefore render as an unexplained stalled ticket with no counterparty, no age against a promise, and no escalation target.** — real pain
  - Who: Any manager whose team sits mid-stack; felt hardest in the week before a release, when the thing not moving is not theirs to move.
  - Evidence: Blocker detection across the field is wired to signals inside the tracked project (blocked status, flags, stale status, unassigned reviewer). The workaround is a Slack DM chain and a mental list of who owes what — commonly a literal side spreadsheet of external dependencies with names and dates.
  - Still unserved because: Genuinely hard plus a pricing conflict: modeling the counterparty requires visibility into boards and repos the buyer may not have access to, and per-seat pricing gives vendors no reason to model people who aren't seats. Enterprise tools sidestep it by selling org-wide, which is exactly what a single manager cannot buy. The tractable slice — treat an external wait as a first-class object with an owner, an age, and a stated ask, populated from what the manager and the tracker already say — is unclaimed.
- **'Completed' is never defined in the manager's terms. A daily report's Yesterday section conflates ticket-transitioned, PR-merged, and actually-shipped, and the manager repeats it upward — then gets burned when Done work turns out to be sitting behind an unreleased branch or a feature flag. No product in this tier carries a deploy signal, so the most-quoted claim in the whole report is the least grounded.** — real pain
  - Who: Managers reporting status to a PM or stakeholder, weekly; catastrophically at release time.
  - Evidence: Git-side tools stop at merge; Jira-side reporting stops at status transition; deployment-aware products are a separate category (Sleuth and similar) that a 1–3 team manager isn't buying. The workaround is asking in Slack 'is that actually out?' before every status meeting.
  - Still unserved because: Partly overlooked, partly a scope decision — deploy/CI ingestion is a fourth integration with wildly heterogeneous pipelines, and vendors would rather claim credit for a merge than qualify it. Nobody has to build the whole pipeline story to fix the honesty problem, though: labelling each completion with which threshold it actually crossed costs nothing and removes a recurring credibility failure.
- **Nothing in the category is priced or shaped for one manager. Per-developer seats, annual contracts, sales-led onboarding and taxonomy implementation mean a manager with twelve engineers and no budget authority cannot buy anything here — so that entire tier is served only by human-reported standup bots with no ground truth from Git or Jira.** — dealbreaker
  - Who: First-time and second-time EMs, tech leads, engineering managers at sub-100-engineer companies — the majority of managers by headcount, and the ones with the least support.
  - Evidence: The field splits cleanly: analytics platforms priced for VP/CTO buyers at 100+ engineers, and cheap self-serve Slack bots that only echo what people type. Nothing occupies the middle, so the actual stack for this persona is a free Jira dashboard plus Geekbot plus a spreadsheet.
  - Still unserved because: Structural, not accidental. Per-seat ARR math makes SMB unprofitable through a sales motion, and the implementation-heavy onboarding these products need cannot be recovered from a small contract. Serving this tier requires zero-touch onboarding, credit-card self-serve, and useful output before any mapping work — a different company shape than any incumbent has. That makes it the most durable opening in the category and the hardest one for incumbents to answer by shipping a feature.
- **Coming back after being away. Dailies are day-shaped and archives are a reverse-chronological list of them, so after a week off, parental leave, or just a skipped Thursday and Friday, there is no 'here is what changed while you were gone and what needs you today' — only ten unread reports to skim.** — real pain
  - Who: Every manager after any absence; also the manager covering someone else's team during that absence, and anyone returning from a long leave to a team that reorganized while they were out.
  - Evidence: No product in the field tracks whether you read the last report, so nothing can compute a delta over an arbitrary window. The workaround is scrolling a Slack channel and opening yesterday's plus last Tuesday's report side by side to diff them mentally.
  - Still unserved because: Overlooked rather than hard — it needs per-reader read state and a memory model, both cheap once you have accumulated org memory. Dashboards are stateless over a date range and standup bots don't know who read what, so neither architecture makes the question expressible. This is the clearest low-cost, high-gratitude gap on the list.

## Also considered (not building now)

- **Unchanged-item collapse (state-fingerprint change discipline)** — Anything the report already told you that hasn't changed appears as one aging line, not a repeated paragraph — the daily shrinks on quiet days instead of restating itself until the manager mutes it. _(impact 4/5, effort 2/5)_
- **External Wait Ledger** — Blockers outside the manager's teams — platform, design, security review, a vendor, another manager's roadmap — become tracked objects with a named counterparty, an age, the specific ask, and a promise date, instead of an unexplained stalled ticket. _(impact 4/5, effort 3/5)_
- **1:1 Prep Brief with commitment carry-forward** — A private, per-person, developmental brief the manager opens 15 minutes before a 1:1: what this person shipped since the last one, what they're stuck on, what they said they'd do last time and whether it happened, and a few things worth asking about — with no comparison to anyone else, ever. _(impact 5/5, effort 5/5)_
- **Recommendation Outcome Loop** — Compass follows up on its own advice — whether the accepted recommendation was carried out, whether the thing it predicted would improve improved — and tells the manager. _(impact 4/5, effort 4/5)_
- **Corrections Notice** — When new data proves a published claim wrong, the next report says so, and the archived report carries a linked correction — an old permalink can never quietly keep lying to a skip-level. _(impact 3/5, effort 3/5)_
- **Welcome-Back Digest (state diff since you last read)** — After leave, holiday, or two skipped days, one report answering "what changed while you were gone and what needs you today" instead of ten unread dailies. _(impact 3/5, effort 3/5)_
